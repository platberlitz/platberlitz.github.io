Nagito Komaeda · Opening scene

The tide drags out before sunrise, and Nagito Komaeda is already knee-deep in the shallows with a plastic bucket hooked over one wrist. Pink light runs flat across the water. He bends, straightens, bends again, dropping shards of bottle glass into the bucket one at a time, pale hair stuck to his temple with sea damp. His green coat is folded over a rock behind him; without it the white shirt hangs loose off his shoulders, and he looks even thinner than usual.

"Ah. Good morning." He straightens fully, holding up a curve of brown glass to the light like a jeweler appraising a stone. "Don't worry, I'm not planning anything ominous. Cleaning is the one thing someone like me can do perfectly, so I thought I'd be useful before everyone woke up. Glass in the sand is bad luck waiting to happen." His mouth quirks. "And I'd know."

The bucket is half full. He sets it above the tideline with the careful precision of someone who has nothing else to do with his hands, then stands there looking out at the horizon, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms narrow and pale.

"Everyone's afraid right now. I can feel it at breakfast, all that talent sitting in one room doing nothing with itself." His voice stays pleasant, almost idle. "It's a waste. Hope like theirs should be tested. Pushed. It's like watching a loaded spring nobody's allowed to touch."

He turns, and for a second the grey-green of his eyes seems to swirl, there and gone.

"Sorry. It's too early for that kind of talk." He retrieves the bucket and steps up onto the dry sand, close enough that the smell of salt and clean laundry comes off him. "Actually, since you're here... I found something strange washed up near the rocks. I was going to keep it to myself until I understood it better, but you're here, so maybe that's the luck deciding for me." He tilts his head, smile thin and bright. "Will you come look? I'd rather have a witness. For obvious reasons."

"Here. I didn't touch it much, obviously. Something that washes up on the day everyone arrives... that's usually an invitation, and I didn't want to accept it on anyone's behalf."

It's a keycard. Hotel keycard, the cheap plastic kind, except it's crusted edge to edge in dried salt like frost, and the salt keeps regrowing where he brushed it. Stamped in the corner: HOPE'S PEAK FIELD TRIP — GUEST. The room number reads 13. There is no thirteenth room on any of the islands; Nagito says he checked the directory at the front desk twice while everyone was fighting over beach towels.

The strange part, the part he wants a witness for, is the temperature. He picks it up and lays it flat across Kris's palm without ceremony. It weighs maybe forty grams. It is warm. Not sun-warm — skin-warm, the exact temperature of the inside of someone's hand, and it holds that heat steady while they stand there with the wind coming in cold off the water.

"Room thirteen. Which doesn't exist, which means either the card is wrong, or the hotel is keeping a room it hasn't told us about." His thin fingers hover near it without touching. "Or — and this is the one I'd bet on, and my bets are unfortunately meaningful — it hasn't kept it yet."

He smiles, apologetic, tilting his head.

"Sorry. I know how that sounds. But you're the psychologist; you're better placed than me to say whether a warm keycard is a message or just litter with good circulation. I only found it. What's done with it..." He steps back, giving Kris the card and the whole decision, hands folded behind him like a man at a museum. "...that should probably be someone else's luck."

The salt on the plastic crackles faintly, very quietly, the way ice does in a glass.

The card slides along the wallpaper and the wallpaper gives. Not cuts, not tears — gives, like bread. The plastic sinks in to half its depth, and around it the beige paper splits along a seam that was definitely not there a moment ago, peeling back in a neat meter-wide rectangle. Behind it: a door. Dark green, matching Nagito's coat almost exactly, with a brass plate screwed on at eye height. The plate reads 13 in a font the hotel has never used anywhere else.

"Ah." Nagito's breath catches, and it isn't fear. His eyes have picked up that faint swirl, grey-green turning like slow water. "There it is. You called it — buildings do love being poked. I should write that down."

The handle is warm too. It turns without resistance, and the door swings inward onto a room that does not fit in the meter and a half of wall it occupies — it keeps going, four meters, five, a full hotel room receding into a soft yellow light that comes from no lamp anyone can see.

Through the corridor window beside them, the outdoors is conducting its own business.

He steps up to the threshold without crossing it, hands folded behind his back, and looks into the warm yellow room. The far wall holds a bed made with hospital corners.
