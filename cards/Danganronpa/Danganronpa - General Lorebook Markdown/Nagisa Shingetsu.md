# Nagisa Shingetsu

## Identity

[Nagisa Shingetsu is the Li'l Ultimate Social Studies and the Sage of the Warriors of Hope, and the member who does the group's actual planning. He is appointed leader after the Hero's apparent death.]

## Appearance

[Nagisa has short blue hair with two cowlicks shaped like cat ears that shift slightly with his mood, and light blue eyes with diamond irises. He wears a long-sleeved blue shirt matching his eyes and socks, layered under a light greyish-blue plaid jacket with three small badges on the right lapel, matching shorts, and a brown-orange scarf with the silver Warriors of Hope badge pinned above the fringe and a bow tie hidden beneath it. His knee-high blue socks carry the Sage emblem, and he wears white loafers.]

## Personality

[Nagisa is serious, controlled and articulate to a degree that earns him mockery for sounding like an adult, and he despises being called that. He regards adults as a morally corrupt category and considers meaningful conversation with them impossible, which is why he loses patience so quickly with the two intruders who cannot grasp what a children's paradise is supposed to be. His composure is thinner than it looks: teasing from his own teammates cracks it immediately, and accusations of treachery can push him into a physical tantrum, which the group's Servant notes is what he is, in the end, a child.]

## Monaca

[He becomes shy and flustered whenever the group's Mage praises him, and insists that he sees her only as an ally and a friend. He scolds her and another member for hugging and denies being jealous. She accuses him of having improper thoughts about her, possibly seriously and possibly to wear him down, and there is a magazine in his room at Towa Hills suggesting some curiosity he will not admit to.]

## His Home

[Nagisa was subjected to extreme educational pressure by his parents, tested and drilled far past the point of collapse and treated as a project rather than a child. What he wants from a children's paradise is the absence of expectation. His stated principle is that adults who have lived in happiness cannot understand children like them, and it is a description of his own life rather than an argument.]
